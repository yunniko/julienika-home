# Lye, superfat, and why soap recipes are calculated rather than copied

Soap is the product of a chemical reaction between fats and a strong alkali.
That single fact explains most of what beginners find confusing about soap
recipes: why you cannot swap one oil for another by eye, why every recipe
quotes a different amount of lye, and why "a bit extra oil" is a deliberate
design choice rather than sloppiness.

**Safety first, because this is the part that actually hurts you.** Sodium
hydroxide and potassium hydroxide are caustic. They burn skin and eyes, and
the reaction with water is strongly exothermic — the solution can reach near
boiling within seconds and releases fumes worth avoiding. Always add lye to
water, never water to lye, which would let the first contact boil violently and
spit. Wear sealed goggles and gloves, cover your arms and feet, and work where
there is airflow and where children and pets are not. Take the same care
weighing dry flakes as mixing the solution — the dust is caustic too.

**Containers matter more than people expect.** Lye attacks aluminium, zinc and
tin, releasing flammable hydrogen gas, so no aluminium pans, no galvanised or
tin-plated steel, and no aluminium utensils. Use stainless steel, or heavy
plastic that is specifically HDPE (recycling mark 2) or polypropylene (mark 5).
Avoid glass: hot caustic solution etches it over time, and a weakened glass
vessel full of near-boiling lye is a serious accident waiting to happen.

**If it gets on you.** Flush the area immediately with plenty of running water
and keep flushing — this is one where more water and more time is simply
better. For eyes, irrigate immediately and get medical attention urgently. Do
not try to neutralise a burn with vinegar or lemon juice: it is a folk remedy
that adds an acid–base reaction, and its heat, to already injured skin. Water
first, always. Keep the tap accessible before you start, not after.

## Saponification: what the lye is for

Fats are triglycerides — a glycerol backbone carrying three fatty acids. The
hydroxide splits those bonds, producing the salt of each fatty acid (soap) and
free glycerol (glycerine, which stays in the bar and is part of why handmade
soap feels different from many commercial bars).

Which alkali you use decides what kind of soap you get. Sodium hydroxide
(NaOH) makes hard bar soap. Potassium hydroxide (KOH) makes soft or liquid
soap. They are not interchangeable, and a recipe written for one is wrong for
the other.

## SAP values: why every oil needs a different amount of lye

Each fat has its own molecular makeup, so each requires a different mass of
alkali to saponify fully. That requirement is the fat's **saponification
value**, usually given as the grams of hydroxide needed per gram of fat.

Coconut oil needs substantially more lye per gram than olive oil, for example,
because its fatty acids are shorter and therefore more numerous per unit
weight. This is why substituting oils without recalculating is genuinely
dangerous rather than merely inexact: swap coconut oil for olive at the same
weight and you are left with excess unreacted lye in the bar, which is
caustic. Swap the other way and you get a greasy bar that may never firm up.

A further wrinkle for liquid soap: KOH is not normally sold pure. Technical
grade is commonly around 90% pure, with the rest mostly water and carbonate,
so recipes and calculators either assume a purity figure or ask you for one. If
you use a 100% figure against 90% flakes, your soap will be under-alkalised.

Correcting for it is one division: actual weight = ideal weight ÷ purity. If
the calculation calls for 90 g of pure KOH and your flakes are 90% pure, you
weigh out 100 g. Purity varies by supplier, roughly 85–95%, so it is worth
knowing what you have rather than assuming.

## Superfat: deliberately leaving oil unreacted

If you add exactly enough lye to saponify every last gram of fat, any error in
measurement, any variation in the oils, or a SAP value that is an average
rather than an exact figure, can leave you with leftover lye. Soap makers
therefore use less lye than the theoretical maximum, so a small fraction of the
oils survives unsaponified. This is **superfatting**, also called a lye
discount.

Five percent is the usual starting point, meaning about 95% of the theoretical
lye requirement is used. It provides a safety margin against measurement error
and leaves free oil in the bar, which most people find gentler on skin. Higher
superfats — 8% or more — feel richer but soften the bar and shorten its shelf
life, since free oils are what eventually go rancid. Very low superfats are
used for laundry and cleaning bars where mildness matters less.

Note that superfat does not let you choose *which* oil remains free. In cold
process soap, saponification does not neatly reserve your expensive shea
butter; the free fraction reflects the mixture. Claims that a specific luxury
oil is "the superfat" are marketing rather than chemistry, unless the oil is
added after the reaction has substantially finished.

## Water: the variable that changes everything except safety

Water does not participate in saponification's stoichiometry — it is the
solvent that lets the lye meet the fats. That means water content is a process
choice, and recipes express it in several incompatible ways:

- **Water as a percentage of oils** — the traditional way, often around 33%.
  Simple, but it makes lye concentration drift as the recipe changes.
- **Water:lye ratio** — for example 2:1, meaning two parts water per part lye.
- **Lye concentration** — the percentage of lye in the lye solution, commonly
  somewhere around 28–33%.

The last two are directly convertible and are what most experienced makers use,
because they pin the thing that actually matters: how concentrated the solution
is. More water gives a longer working time and a softer initial bar that needs
longer to lose its moisture; less water speeds up trace and firming, and is
usual when a recipe wants to be unmoulded quickly.

## Trace, cure, and why waiting is not optional

Trace is the point where the mixture emulsifies and thickens enough that a
drizzle leaves a momentary trail on the surface. It signals that the oils and
lye solution will no longer separate, not that the soap is finished.

The bulk of saponification happens over the following day or so, but
cold-process soap is normally cured for around four to six weeks before use.
Curing is mostly water evaporating: the bar becomes harder, milder and much
longer-lasting in the shower. A bar used after a week is not usually dangerous
if the recipe was correct — it is simply soft, and dissolves quickly.

Confirming that a batch is safely saponified is where a lot of well-meant
advice goes wrong. The intuitive move is to reach for pH strips, but they are
the weaker test: soapmaking references describe strips and phenolphthalein as
notoriously inaccurate on soap, and the industry's actual method for detecting
excess alkali is a total alkalinity titration, not a pH reading.

The reason is that pH is the wrong question. Finished soap is alkaline by
nature — properly made, skin-safe soap ranges from roughly 9.5 to 11.5
depending on the fatty acids in the recipe — so a high reading tells you almost
nothing. What you actually want to know is whether *free, unreacted alkali*
remains, and a soap slurry's pH is dominated by the soap itself.

That is why the old "zap test" survives: touching a damp fingertip that has
been swiped on the bar to your tongue detects free alkali as an unmistakable
sensation like a nine-volt battery. Wait two or three days after making, since
fresh soap zaps before saponification has finished, and **swipe with a finger —
do not put the bar against your tongue**, which risks a chemical burn if the
batch really is lye-heavy.

A bar that zaps goes back to be rebatched or thrown away. A bar that does not,
made from a correctly calculated recipe, is fine.

## Running the numbers

Every batch needs its own calculation: the oils you actually used, their SAP
values, your chosen superfat, and your water figure. The
[soap lye calculator](https://soap-lye-calculator.svc.julienika.cz) on this
site does exactly that, including the water:lye and concentration conversions
and a per-oil SAP reference.

Whatever you calculate with, weigh everything — including the water — on a
scale, and recalculate whenever the oils change. Soap making is forgiving about
fragrance, colour and technique, and unforgiving about the ratio of alkali to
fat.
