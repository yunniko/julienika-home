# Baker's percentage and hydration: how bread recipes actually scale

Most bread recipes are written for one loaf, in whatever units the author
happened to use. That works until you want two loaves, or you have 700 g of
flour left in the bag instead of the 500 g the recipe assumes, or you want to
compare two recipes that look nothing alike. Baker's percentage is the
convention bakers use to make recipes comparable and scalable, and once it
clicks it is hard to go back to cup measurements.

## The one rule: flour is always 100%

In baker's percentage, every ingredient is expressed as a percentage of the
total flour weight, and the flour itself is defined as 100%. A recipe with
1000 g flour, 700 g water, 20 g salt and 200 g starter is written as:

- Flour: 100%
- Water: 70%
- Salt: 2%
- Starter: 20%

Those percentages add up to more than 100%, which surprises people at first.
That is expected: the percentages are not shares of the total dough, they are
ratios against flour. The number that matters most, and the one people argue
about, is the water percentage — the dough's **hydration**.

## What hydration actually changes

Hydration is water weight divided by flour weight, times 100. A dough with
1000 g flour and 700 g water is at 70% hydration. The percentage is a good
predictor of how the dough will behave:

- **Around 60–65%** — a stiff dough that holds its shape, kneads easily and
  stays where you put it. Typical of sandwich loaves, bagels and many enriched
  doughs. The crumb tends to be even and fine.
- **Around 68–75%** — the range most rustic sourdough recipes live in. The
  dough is slack enough to need folds rather than heavy kneading, and open
  enough to give an irregular crumb.
- **Above 80%** — a very wet dough that spreads if it is not supported. It can
  produce a dramatically open crumb, but it is genuinely harder to shape, and
  it punishes under-developed gluten and over-proofing.

None of these boundaries are laws. Flour matters as much as the number: whole
grain and freshly milled flours absorb noticeably more water than white bread
flour, so 75% hydration in wholemeal can feel stiffer than 70% in white. A
recipe's hydration figure is only directly comparable to another recipe using
similar flour.

## The part most people get wrong: the starter counts

A sourdough starter is flour and water too, and if you ignore that, your real
hydration is not what you think it is. A starter kept at 100% hydration — equal
weights of flour and water, the most common convention — is half flour and half
water by weight. So 200 g of it contributes 100 g flour and 100 g water to the
dough.

Take the recipe above: 1000 g flour, 700 g water, 200 g of 100% starter. The
*stated* hydration is 70%, but the true totals are 1100 g flour and 800 g
water, which is 72.7% hydration. That gap is small here, and large in recipes
that lean on a big proportion of starter.

This is also why a stiff starter changes the maths. A starter fed at 50%
hydration (half as much water as flour) is two-thirds flour, one-third water,
so the same 200 g contributes about 133 g flour and 67 g water. Swapping one
starter for the other without adjusting will move your dough by several
percentage points.

The convention of quoting hydration without the starter is not wrong, it is
just a convention — but when a recipe behaves differently than its number
suggests, this is usually the reason.

## Scaling a recipe without breaking it

Because every ingredient is pinned to flour, scaling is arithmetic rather than
guesswork. Two situations come up constantly.

**You know how much flour you have.** Multiply each percentage by your flour
weight. With 700 g of flour at the percentages above: water 490 g, salt 14 g,
starter 140 g.

**You know how much dough you want.** This is the more common case — a tin or a
banneton takes a certain weight. Add all the percentages together to get the
total as a proportion of flour. For 100 + 70 + 2 + 20, that total is 192%. Then
divide the target dough weight by that figure to recover the flour weight. For
a 900 g loaf: 900 ÷ 1.92 ≈ 469 g flour, and every other ingredient follows from
its percentage.

Add a few percent for dough left behind on the bowl and hands if you need the
finished weight to be exact.

## Salt, and why 2% is the number you keep seeing

Salt is almost always quoted between 1.8% and 2.2% of flour weight. It is not
only seasoning: salt tightens the gluten network and slows fermentation, so
cutting it substantially changes the dough's timing and handling, not just the
flavour. At 1000 g flour, 2% is 20 g — a figure worth weighing rather than
estimating, because a teaspoon of coarse salt and a teaspoon of fine salt are
quite different weights.

## Why weight, not volume

Baker's percentage only works with weights. A cup of flour can vary by a
substantial margin depending on whether the flour was scooped or spooned and
how settled it was, and that variation lands directly in your hydration. A
kitchen scale that reads in single grams removes the problem, and it is the
single change that most improves consistency for people who bake regularly.

## Working it out

If you would rather not do the arithmetic by hand, the
[sourdough calculators](https://sourdough.svc.julienika.cz) on this site cover
the three cases above: finding a dough's hydration, scaling a full recipe from
either flour weight or target dough weight, and working out starter feeding
ratios.

The underlying maths is simple enough to do on paper, though, and knowing where
the numbers come from is what lets you judge whether a recipe you found
somewhere is going to behave the way its author says it will.
