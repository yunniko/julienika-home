# Clay shrinkage: why your pot comes out smaller than you made it

Every ceramic piece leaves the kiln smaller than it was on the wheel. If you
have ever thrown a mug to fit a particular lid, or made a tile to fill an exact
gap, you have discovered this the expensive way. Shrinkage is predictable, but
only if you measure it for the clay you actually use rather than trusting a
number from a book.

## Clay shrinks twice, for two different reasons

**Drying shrinkage** happens before the kiln is involved. Plastic clay is
mineral particles separated by a film of water. As that water evaporates, the
particles pull together and the piece contracts. This is where most warping and
cracking originates, because the outside dries first and tries to shrink around
a still-wet interior. Rims, handles and thin edges lose water fastest, which is
why they crack first and why potters slow drying down with plastic or damp
cloth.

By the time the piece is bone dry it has already given up most of its
dimensional change, and it will not shrink further while it sits on the shelf.

**Firing shrinkage** happens in the kiln, and it is chemistry rather than
evaporation. Bisque firing drives off chemically bound water and burns out
organics; it produces relatively little shrinkage and leaves the piece porous
and absorbent, which is precisely what makes it easy to glaze. The large
change comes in the glaze or high firing, when the clay body begins to
vitrify — glassy phases form between the particles and pull the structure
tighter as porosity falls.

This is why the same clay fired to a higher cone shrinks more. Shrinkage and
vitrification are two views of the same process, which is also why a body fired
well past its intended range slumps and deforms: it is over-vitrified.

## Typical numbers, and why you should not trust them

Total shrinkage, wet to fired, commonly lands somewhere around 10–14% for many
stoneware and porcelain bodies, with earthenware often lower. Porcelains tend
to sit at the high end and can exceed that range.

That spread is far too wide to size anything precisely. A 12% figure applied to
a clay that actually shrinks 9% will leave a 300 mm platter about 10 mm off —
enough to ruin a piece made to fit something. Manufacturers publish shrinkage
for their bodies, and those figures are a reasonable starting point, but your
result depends on your firing schedule, your kiln's real temperature, and how
wet the clay was when you measured.

## Measuring your own shrinkage rate

The test is simple and worth doing once per clay body and firing schedule.

Roll out a flat test bar of the clay, and while it is still plastic, mark a
line exactly 100 mm long on it. Use a needle tool and a ruler, and mark
crisply — the precision of this line is the precision of everything that
follows. Note which way the bar was rolled, since some clays shrink slightly
differently along and across the direction of working.

Let the bar dry completely, fire it through your normal bisque and glaze
schedule, then measure the marks again. If your 100 mm line now measures 88 mm,
total shrinkage is 12%.

The general form, for any starting length:

**Shrinkage % = (wet length − fired length) ÷ wet length × 100**

Measure the dry bar before firing too, and you separate drying shrinkage from
firing shrinkage, which tells you whether cracking problems belong to your
drying routine or your kiln.

## Sizing a piece to finish at a given dimension

This is the calculation people actually need, and it is where the intuitive
approach fails. If a piece shrinks 12%, you do not make it 12% bigger — adding
12% to the finished size and then removing 12% of that larger number does not
return you to where you started.

The correct relation is:

**Wet size = finished size ÷ (1 − shrinkage as a decimal)**

For a 150 mm finished bowl in a clay that shrinks 12%: 150 ÷ 0.88 ≈ 170.5 mm.
Making it 150 + 12% = 168 mm would leave you roughly 2.5 mm short — small, but
exactly the kind of error that stops a lid from fitting.

The same formula applies to any linear dimension: height, diameter, the spacing
between two features. It does not apply directly to volume or weight, which
scale with the cube of the linear change. A piece that shrinks 12% linearly
loses around 32% of its volume, which is why a mug thrown to hold 400 ml of
water at the wet stage will hold noticeably less when fired.

## Practical consequences worth planning for

- **Lids and fitted parts** should be made from the same clay, at the same
  time, and fired together. Two bodies with different shrinkage rates will not
  fit each other, and neither will two firings that ran differently.
- **Attachments** — handles, spouts, sprigs — should be at a similar moisture
  level to the piece they join. A wet handle on a leather-hard mug shrinks more
  than its host and pulls itself off at the join.
- **Flat pieces** shrink too, and they do it while sitting on a shelf that does
  not. Tiles and platters crack when friction fights the contraction, which is
  why potters fire them on sand, grog or a wash rather than bare shelf.
- **Repeat work** needs a recorded shrinkage figure per body. Write it on the
  bag; do not rediscover it each time.

## Doing the arithmetic

The [clay shrinkage calculators](https://clay-shrinkage-calculator.svc.julienika.cz)
on this site cover the three directions of this problem: working out a
shrinkage percentage from a test bar, predicting a fired size from a wet one,
and calculating the wet size needed to finish at a target dimension.

They are only as good as the shrinkage figure you feed them, though — which
brings it back to the test bar. Ten minutes of measuring once tells you more
than any published number for the clay in your studio.
