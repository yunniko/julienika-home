# Aquarium stocking: what bioload actually means, and why the inch-per-gallon
rule fails

Nearly every beginner meets the same guideline: one inch of fish per gallon of
water. It is memorable, easy to apply, and wrong often enough to cause real
problems. Understanding what it is trying to approximate — and where it breaks
down — is the difference between a stable tank and a permanent struggle.

## The nitrogen cycle is the actual constraint

Fish excrete ammonia, continuously, through their gills as well as their waste.
Uneaten food and decaying plant matter add more. Ammonia is acutely toxic to
fish at low concentrations, so in a sealed box of water it would quickly become
lethal.

One subtlety changes how you read a test result. Ammonia exists in water in two
forms: un-ionized NH₃, which is the acutely toxic one, and the ammonium ion
NH₄⁺, which is far less so. Hobby test kits measure the two together as total
ammonia, and the toxic share is not measured at all but calculated from pH and
temperature. Higher pH shifts the balance towards the toxic form, sharply;
higher temperature pushes the same way more gently. So an identical reading is a
far more urgent problem in a hard, alkaline rift-lake tank than in a soft,
acidic one. Never read the ammonia number without knowing your pH.

What makes an aquarium possible is a bacterial colony, living mostly in the
filter media and on surfaces throughout the tank, that converts ammonia to
nitrite, and a second population that converts nitrite to nitrate. Nitrite is
also toxic — it interferes with the blood's ability to carry oxygen, which is
why a nitrite spike is an emergency and why adding chloride, as salt, is the
standard stopgap while the underlying cause is fixed. Nitrate is far less
toxic, and is removed by water changes and, to a degree, by live plants.

The organisms are worth naming correctly, because the hobby literature has been
slow to update: in freshwater filters the nitrite oxidizers are largely
*Nitrospira* rather than the commonly credited *Nitrobacter*, and some
*Nitrospira* carry out the whole conversion from ammonia to nitrate by
themselves. It is one reason bottled bacteria products vary so much in how well
they work.

"Bioload" is simply the rate at which a tank's inhabitants produce waste,
measured against the capacity of that bacterial colony and filter to process
it. A tank is overstocked when production outruns processing — not when the
fish look crowded.

This is why a new tank must be **cycled** before it is stocked. Establishing
that bacterial colony takes weeks, and adding a full complement of fish to an
uncycled tank exposes them to ammonia and nitrite spikes. Adding fish
gradually, rather than all at once, lets the colony grow to match.

The humane way to do it is a **fishless cycle**: dose pure ammonia to a target
concentration and wait, testing, until the tank converts that dose to nitrate
within a day and both ammonia and nitrite read zero. Seeding the filter with
media, gravel or a sponge from an established tank shortens it considerably,
because you are importing the bacteria rather than waiting for them to arrive.

**Two things kill that colony after you have built it.** Tap water in most
places is treated with chlorine or chloramine, both of which are effective
disinfectants and therefore effective bacteria killers — water changes need a
dechlorinator, and chloramine additionally releases ammonia as it is broken
down, which your filter then has to process. The second is washing filter media
under a running tap: rinse it in a bucket of water taken from the tank instead.
More cycled tanks are crashed by these two habits than by overstocking.

## Why the inch-per-gallon rule misleads

The rule treats fish as interchangeable units of length. Several things break
that assumption.

**Mass does not scale with length.** A fish twice as long is far more than
twice the volume, and produces waste roughly in proportion to its mass, not its
length. Ten 1-inch tetras and one 10-inch fish are not remotely equivalent
bioloads.

**Adult size is what counts.** Fish are sold small. A common pleco in a shop
tank is a few centimetres long and may exceed 40 cm as an adult; goldfish
routinely outgrow the bowls they are sold for. Stocking must be planned for the
adult animal, not the one in the bag.

**Species differ in output.** Goldfish and cichlids are notably messy;
similarly sized fish with different diets and metabolisms are not.

**Tank shape matters as much as volume.** Gas exchange happens at the water
surface, so a wide, shallow tank supports more respiration than a tall narrow
one of identical volume. Footprint also determines whether territorial or
bottom-dwelling fish have the floor space they need; a 100-litre tall column
does not suit a fish that patrols horizontally.

**Filtration and maintenance vary hugely.** A heavily planted tank with an
oversized filter and weekly water changes supports a load that would crash a
bare tank with a small internal filter.

## Behaviour is a stocking constraint too

Water chemistry is only half the question. A tank can be biologically
understocked and still be a bad home.

- **Schooling species** need numbers. Many popular tetras, rasboras, danios and
  corydoras are shoaling fish that show stress behaviours in small groups —
  typically six is quoted as a working minimum, and more is usually better.
- **Territory** matters for cichlids, many catfish and anything that claims a
  cave. Two males in a tank with one defensible spot will fight regardless of
  the litres available.
- **Compatibility** covers temperament and size together. Anything small enough
  to fit in a larger tank mate's mouth eventually will, and fin-nippers make
  poor companions for long-finned species.
- **Water parameters** must actually overlap. Fish from soft, acidic water and
  those from hard, alkaline water can both be healthy, but rarely in the same
  tank.

## A more useful way to think about it

Rather than a single number, work through the questions in order:

1. **What is the tank's real volume?** Subtract the freeboard you do not fill
   and the displacement of substrate and hardscape — together commonly 10–20%
   less than the nominal figure on the box.
2. **What is each species' adult size, and its minimum group size?** Multiply
   those out before anything else.
3. **Does the footprint suit them?** Check length and width against the
   swimming style, not just litres.
4. **Is the filtration matched to the load**, with turnover appropriate for the
   species — some fish dislike strong current.
5. **What maintenance are you actually going to do?** Honest weekly capacity,
   not aspirational.
6. **Do the parameters and temperaments overlap?**

Then stock gradually, and let your test results tell you whether the load is
sustainable. In an established tank, **ammonia and nitrite should both read
zero** — any persistent reading above that is a problem to solve now, not a
number to tolerate. Nitrate is the one that accumulates between water changes,
and most community-tank guidance puts the working ceiling somewhere in the
20–40 mg/l region, lower for sensitive species.

Test your tap water before drawing conclusions from any of this. Mains water in
agricultural areas can carry substantial nitrate of its own — the EU drinking
water limit is 50 mg/l — and if it does, water changes cannot bring the tank
below that figure, and what looks like overstocking is really your source
water.

Rising nitrate that needs ever-larger water changes to control is the signal
that a tank is carrying more than it comfortably can.

## Estimating it

The [aquarium stocking calculator](https://aquarium-stocking-calculator.svc.julienika.cz)
on this site works through tank volume, species-aware bioload and compatibility
rather than a flat length rule, and includes a reference chart of adult sizes
and minimum group sizes.

Treat any such estimate as a starting point for judgement, not a verdict. The
tank in front of you — its plants, its filter, its maintenance routine, and the
particular animals in it — is the real test, and a test kit tells you more
about your stocking level than any formula can.
