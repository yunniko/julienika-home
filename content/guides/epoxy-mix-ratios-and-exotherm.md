# Epoxy mix ratios and exotherm: why "a bit more hardener" ruins a pour

Epoxy is unusual among the materials hobbyists work with, in that it gives you
almost no room to improvise. Add extra hardener to speed it up, as you might
add extra catalyst to polyester resin, and you do not get a faster cure — you
get a batch that never cures properly at all. Understanding why makes the rest
of the rules obvious.

## The ratio is chemistry, not a recipe

Epoxy cures by a reaction between two different molecules: the resin, carrying
epoxide groups, and the hardener, typically an amine carrying reactive hydrogen
sites. Each resin site pairs with a hardener site to build a single large
cross-linked network. That is what turns a liquid into a rigid solid.

The ratio a manufacturer specifies is the one that supplies roughly matching
numbers of these sites. Deviate, and the surplus component has nothing left to
react with. It does not evaporate or eventually harden; it stays in the
network as unreacted material, permanently. The result is the classic failed
pour: soft or tacky patches, a surface that stays slightly sticky, reduced
strength, and sometimes a cloudy finish. There is no rescue — it has to be
scraped off and redone.

Polyester and some other resins use a true catalyst, where the amount changes
the *speed* of the reaction rather than its stoichiometry. This is why advice
carried over from fibreglass work goes so badly wrong with epoxy. If you want a
faster epoxy cure, you change the hardener grade or the temperature, never the
ratio.

## Weight and volume ratios are different numbers

A ratio only means something when you know what is being measured. Resin and
hardener usually have different densities, so the same mix expressed by volume
and by weight gives different figures — a system that is 1:1 by volume may be
something like 100:83 by weight.

Manufacturers publish one or both, and the two are not interchangeable. Mixing
a weight ratio using measuring cups, or a volume ratio on a scale, is a common
and expensive mistake. Check which one your product's data sheet states, and
measure in that unit.

Weight is generally the easier of the two to get right: a digital scale reading
in single grams beats eyeballing graduated cups, particularly for small
batches. Volume measuring is workable, but it needs cups with genuine
graduations and enough patience to let thick resin settle and drain.

Tolerances are tighter than people expect. Many systems want accuracy within a
few percent, and small batches are where errors creep in — 5 g of error matters
enormously in a 50 g mix and not at all in a 5 kg one. If you need a tiny
quantity, mixing a slightly larger batch and discarding the excess is often
more reliable than trying to weigh two grams of hardener accurately.

## Mixing is part of the ratio

A correctly measured batch that is poorly mixed behaves like an incorrectly
measured one, because locally the ratio is wrong. Scrape the sides and the
bottom of the container thoroughly — unmixed resin clinging to the wall is the
usual culprit behind a sticky patch in an otherwise good pour. Many workers
transfer the mix to a second clean container partway through and continue
mixing there, which leaves any unmixed film behind in the first cup.

Mix deliberately rather than vigorously. Whipping air into the resin creates
bubbles you then have to remove, and heat from friction shortens your working
time.

## Exotherm: the part that can actually be dangerous

The curing reaction is exothermic — it releases heat. In a thin film, that heat
dissipates into the air and the substrate and nothing remarkable happens. In a
deep pour or a large mass left in the mixing cup, the heat cannot escape, and
it accelerates the reaction, which releases more heat, which accelerates it
further.

A runaway exotherm can produce a great deal of heat quickly. In practice this
shows up as resin that yellows, cracks internally, shrinks away from the mould,
smokes, deforms its container, or in bad cases becomes hot enough to be a
genuine burn and fire risk. It is the single most common way a hobby resin
project goes seriously wrong.

Three things drive it: mass, depth, and the formulation. That is why:

- **Deep pours need deep-pour resin.** These systems are formulated to react
  more slowly, and their instructions specify a maximum pour depth — often in
  the range of a few centimetres per layer. General-purpose coating epoxies can
  have maximum depths of only a few millimetres.
- **Layering beats one big pour.** Let each layer cool and cure enough before
  adding the next.
- **Never leave a mixed batch sitting in the cup.** A full cup of standard
  epoxy is a compact mass with nowhere to dump its heat; poured out thin, the
  same quantity is harmless. If you must abandon a batch, spread it out rather
  than binning it as a lump.

Warmer ambient temperatures make all of this more energetic, and cold slows
curing down to the point where some systems will not cure at all below their
minimum working temperature.

## Working out how much you need

Coverage for a flat pour is a volume calculation: area × thickness. A square
metre at 1 mm thick is one litre, which scales conveniently — 2 mm over half a
square metre is the same litre. For moulds, water is a reasonable proxy for
volume if the mould is sealed and you can dry it thoroughly afterwards.

Mix a margin over the calculated figure. Resin left in the cup and on the
stirrer is unavoidable, and running short mid-pour is worse than a little
waste, because a second batch poured onto a partly gelled first one can leave a
visible line.
